Bio

About me

I am a Ph.D. student in Computer Science at The University of Alabama, where I work as a Graduate Assistant in the Graduate School and previously served as a Graduate Teaching Assistant. I have more than five years of experience in teaching and academic roles, and I enjoy helping students build their skills and confidence.

From 2023 to 2025, I was a Lecturer in the Department of CSE at Bangladesh University of Professionals, where I also served as Graduate Coordinator, batch advisor and admission committee member. Before that, I taught at Green University of Bangladesh. The courses I have taught include Structured Programming, Object Oriented Programming, Data Communication, Data Structures and Algorithms.

My current research interests are Brain-Computer Interfacing (BCI) and Human-Computer Interaction (HCI). My earlier work focused on explainable and federated AI for healthcare, including Alzheimer's disease prediction, healthcare IoT and brain signal analysis. I have published 12 peer-reviewed papers in venues such as IEEE Access, PLOS ONE and Expert Systems with Applications.

I earned my B.Sc. and M.Sc. in Information and Communication Engineering from Bangladesh University of Professionals, finishing first in my class in both programs.

Teaching Philosophy

Learning happens when the right environment meets a caring facilitator

Students learn best when a good learning environment is paired with a knowledgeable, caring facilitator.

As an educator, my role is to help students learn and think about something new and interesting, and to connect textbook concepts with real-life examples. I give students opportunities to learn, think and grow their knowledge, and I am always ready to share what I know.

Above all, I believe students are human beings. Each of them comes to class with their own background, struggles, and goals. I care about my students, treat every one of them with respect, and support them patiently as they learn.

1

I teach as a facilitator

I give students room to explore and work through ideas, and I stay available to guide them and share knowledge when they need it.

2

I build on what students already know

Short, easy examples and brief gamified activities connect new course content to students' prior knowledge and experience. Before teaching loops, for example, I have students guide a classmate across the room one instruction at a time, until someone asks for a way to say "repeat."

3

I aim for joyful learning

Real-life scenarios, full use of multimedia, clear concepts and a friendly classroom make lessons enjoyable and memorable.

4

I keep growing as a teacher

I keep deepening my pedagogical knowledge and use of technology, and I work to keep my classroom warm and supportive.

In my classroom

  • Real-life scenariosEach concept is anchored in a situation students recognize.
  • MultimediaSlides, demos and visuals reach students who learn in different ways.
  • Gamified warm-upsQuick, entertaining activities check prior knowledge before new material.
  • A friendly environmentStudents feel safe to ask questions, try ideas and make mistakes.

How I know learning happened

  1. JoyStudents enjoy the work and the room feels alive.
  2. WillingnessStudents volunteer, ask and keep going on their own.
  3. UnderstandingStudents can explain the concept and apply it correctly.

How I understand my own teaching

Completing several teaching inventories helped me describe my approach more clearly. I teach mainly as a facilitator: I ask questions, suggest options and support students while they work through a problem. I also teach by example, showing students how I approach a problem so they can see the thinking as well as the answer. When I lecture, my first concern is organizing the key concepts so that they are clear and easy to follow.

What motivates me most is seeing students use what they learned in my class in other courses and in their jobs, so that is how I judge whether my teaching is working.

I also know where these strengths need care. Too much detail can overwhelm beginners, so I keep my explanations short and focused. New learners often need direct instruction before they can work on their own, so I show first, then guide, then step back. Because I focus so much on learning outcomes, I also make time on purpose to get to know my students.

Course Dossier

Introduction to Programming

My plan for a first college programming course, drawing on my experience teaching Structured Programming and Object Oriented Programming. It is built for students who have never written a line of code and may be nervous to start.

Level
First-year undergraduate
Prerequisites
None
Language
C
Format
15 weeks, lecture with active learning
Learning outcomes and course design

I plan this course backward: I start with what students should be able to do, decide what would prove it, and only then choose the lessons.

By the end of the course, students will be able to:

  1. Read and trace a program, and predict what it will do before running it.
  2. Write and compile C programs using variables, conditionals, loops, functions and arrays.
  3. Break a real-life problem into smaller steps and turn those steps into code.
  4. Find and fix compiler errors and logic errors using a systematic debugging process.
  5. Evaluate AI-generated code for accuracy instead of accepting it without checking.
  6. Explain how their code works to another person.
StageMy plan
1. GoalsStudents think like problem solvers and feel confident learning new tools on their own. Essential questions: How do I tell a computer exactly what I mean? How do I know my program really works? When should I trust AI-generated code?
2. EvidenceWeekly labs (25%), short quizzes (15%), midterm and final exams (30%), a final project built in stages (20%), and class participation (10%).
3. LearningShort mini-lectures, live coding with predictions, pair programming, code tracing, review games and feedback at every stage of the project.

Content delivery: how I spend each class

Every class follows the same 55-minute routine, so students always know what to expect. It mixes lecture with active learning in the ways I showed in my teaching demonstration: a recap and a wrap-up at either end of the class (bookend), guided notes during each lecture (overlay), and a think-pair-share between the two lectures (interleave).

Example lesson: introduction to loops

Week 5. Students already know variables, printf and if/else. By the end of class, they should be able to write a for loop, choose between for, while and do-while, and explain why a loop might never stop.

Activity Quick recap: conditionalsBookend Lecture 1: for loopsOverlay Think-pair-share: trace a loopInterleave Lecture 2: while and do-while loopsOverlay Wrap-up: three key pointsBookend
What I do Show two short questions on if/else from the last class Open with the "human robot": a volunteer walks to the door only when told "take one step," until someone asks for a way to say "repeat." Then explain the three parts of a for loop and run each example. Point out a common mistake: writing i < 5 when you mean i <= 5. Show the loop, walk around to listen to pairs, then trace it on the board during the share Explain how while and do-while differ and when to use each loop: for when you know how many times to repeat, while when it depends on a condition, and do-while when the code must run once, such as a menu. End with a loop that never stops. Collect the responses and read them before the next class; anything missing becomes the next quick recap

Materials and answers 
// 1. What does this print?
int x = 7;
if (x > 5) printf("big");
else printf("small");

// 2. What does this print?
int age = 18;
if (age >= 18 && age < 21) printf("A");
else printf("B");

Answers: big, A.

 
for (int i = 1; i <= 5; i++) {
    printf("%d ", i);       // 1 2 3 4 5
}

int sum = 0;
for (int i = 1; i <= 10; i++) {
    sum += i;
}
printf("%d", sum);          // 55
 
for (int i = 10; i > 0; i -= 3) {
    printf("%d ", i);
}

Answer: 10 7 4 1 (four times). Pairs that finish early rewrite it to count down from 10 to 1, one at a time.

 
int count = 0;
while (count < 3) {
    printf("%d\n", count);
}

Answer: count never changes, so count < 3 is always true. Adding count++ fixes it.

 Points I hope to see:
  • A for loop has a start, a condition and an update.
  • A while loop checks first; a do-while loop always runs at least once.
  • A loop whose condition never becomes false runs forever.
Teaching techniques
  1. Background knowledge probe: a short survey before each unit shows me where to start.
  2. Live coding with predictions: students commit to an answer before I run the code.
  3. Guided notes: partial notes keep students focused on the key ideas.
  4. Think-pair-share: students reason alone, compare with a partner, then share with the class.
  5. Pair programming: partners swap between writing code and reviewing it.
  6. Code tracing: students follow a program line by line to see what it really does.
  7. Parsons problems: students put scrambled lines of code into the correct order.
  8. Invent the quiz: students write test questions and explain the correct answers.
  9. Debug the AI: students find and correct errors in code from an AI tool.
  10. Three key points: at the end of class, students write three things they learned, which shows me what to revisit next time.
Discussion questions
  1. Exploratory: What everyday tasks do you already follow like an algorithm?
  2. Diagnostic: Why does this loop never stop?
  3. Action: Your program crashes the night before a deadline. What do you do first?
  4. Cause and effect: What happens to this program if we move the print statement inside the loop?
  5. Hypothetical: If functions did not exist, how would your project change?
  6. Priority: Which matters most in a first program: speed, correctness or readability? Why?
  7. Evaluative: Two solutions both work. Which is better, and how did you decide?
  8. Relational: How is a function like a recipe, and where does that comparison break down?
  9. Extension: Where else could you use what you learned about arrays?
  10. Summary: What has debugging taught you about how you solve problems?

Teaching with AI

More and more students use AI to study and to complete assignments, so I teach them to question its output instead of accepting it without thinking twice.

CRAFT criteria

  • CredibilityCan this be traced to a reliable source?
  • RelevanceDoes it answer the problem I was given?
  • AccuracyDoes the code compile and give correct results?
  • FairnessDoes it contain bias or leave anyone out?
  • TransparencyCan I explain how it works and show where I used AI?

Before using AI output

  1. PauseDo not copy it right away.
  2. CheckRead it and test it with your own inputs.
  3. ConfirmCompare it with a credible source.
  4. DecideUse it, change it or reject it.

I hold myself to the same standard: I always verify AI output before I present it to students.

Inclusive teaching

Having taught in both Bangladesh and the United States, I know that students bring very different backgrounds into the same classroom. A first programming course can feel closed to anyone who did not grow up around computers, so I design it to welcome true beginners.

  1. I assume no prior experience, and I say so on the first day.
  2. I use free tools and free materials so cost is not a barrier.
  3. I choose examples from many cultures and everyday settings.
  4. I give clear written instructions along with spoken ones, and I define technical terms.
  5. I rotate pair programming roles so that everyone writes code and everyone gets heard.
  6. I treat errors as a normal part of programming, including my own during live coding.

But true beginners often need direct instruction first, so in this course I show, then guide, then step back. As a Fascinator lecturer, I love organizing ideas and sharing detail, but too much detail can overwhelm a new programmer. That is why I keep each mini-lecture short and focused on one idea.

What motivates me. Influence is by far my strongest motivator: I would feel my teaching matters if students used what they learned in my class in other classes and in their jobs. So I judge this course by what students can do at the end, not only by how much they enjoyed it. My lower Connection score reminds me to make time for relationships on purpose, by learning names and inviting students to office hours.

How I will keep growing
